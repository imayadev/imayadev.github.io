Why are South Asian women at greater risk of iron deficiency anaemia, and what ethical responsibilities does the NHS have in addressing this?

By Muntaqa Oishi · Age 17 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 3rd March 2026

Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) is a condition in which low iron levels reduce the production of healthy red blood cells, minimising the amount of oxygen transported around the body. It can cause symptoms such as fatigue, breathlessness and reduced concentration, ultimately leading to a decreased quality of life. South Asian women and girls are disproportionately affected by IDA, both in South Asian countries and within South Asian communities in the UK. This essay will explore iron deficiency anaemia and some reasons for its prevalence in South Asian women, as well as the ethical obligations the NHS has in tackling this inequality.

To understand why South Asian women are at greater risk of iron deficiency anaemia, it is important to first consider how it arises. Iron is the central component of the haem group in the protein haemoglobin, with one iron ion in each of the four haem groups in a haemoglobin molecule. These iron ions are the sites where oxygen binds, forming oxyhaemoglobin, which is how oxygen is delivered around the body. IDA, where there is a lack of iron, means that fewer haem groups can be formed, limiting the amount of functional haemoglobin produced. This results in red blood cells that are often smaller (microcytic) and paler (hypochromic), because they contain less haemoglobin. The blood therefore does not carry a sufficient amount of oxygen around the body.

IDA can develop from several causes, including diets low in iron, blood loss, and poor iron absorption in the small intestine. Iron-rich foods include red meat, eggs, green vegetables, and other foods; however, South Asian communities often follow vegetarian or vegan diets for religious or cultural reasons, which means they are especially vulnerable to IDA caused by inadequate dietary intake of iron. Also, iron exists in two forms: haem- and non-haem iron. Haem-iron is more efficiently absorbed, and is found in animal sources such as red meat, while non-haem iron is less easily absorbed, and so is less bioavailable. Not only does much of the iron intake in South Asian diets come from non-haem sources, the bioavailability of non-haem foods is reduced even further by the regular consumption of traditional drinks like chai. This is because tea is rich in polyphenols, which are compounds that bind to non-haem iron and make it less soluble, preventing its absorption into the blood. Another cause of IDA is blood loss, which may happen from heavy menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, most common in adult men and postmenopausal women, or regularly donating blood. One study found that some South Asian women, notably in the UK, value a heavy menstrual period because a light one is thought to result in weight gain and pain. Misconceptions like this highlight a significant ethical concern: women may choose not to seek treatment for heavy bleeding due to false beliefs about their health, and language and cultural barriers between patients and doctors only make it harder for accurate information to reach those who need it. Additionally, cultural taboos surrounding menstruation cause reluctance to speak about heavy bleeding with family or doctors, especially male doctors, which contributes further to undiagnosed and untreated IDA.

The NHS has clear responsibilities in addressing health inequalities like this one affecting South Asian women with iron deficiency anaemia. Effective yet sensitive communication is crucial, which means that the NHS must go beyond basic translation services, and instead proactively address language needs to ensure that groups like South Asian women can access vital information about IDA. This approach is supported by the NHS Constitution for England, which guarantees patients’ rights to access information they can understand and are comfortable with. Beyond communication, the NHS also has a duty to carry out targeted schemes for high-risk groups, as opposed to universal approaches that fail to address everybody. This could include routine checks like Full Blood Counts for South Asian women during GP appointments, which reflects the NHS principle that healthcare is based on clinical need, as resources would be directed towards those most vulnerable to IDA. While the NHS does currently offer free prescriptions for iron tablets to pregnant women, it is essential that these measures become preventative and not simply reactive by being carried out before IDA can develop.

Ultimately, the NHS has a responsibility to address iron deficiency anaemia in South Asian women, implementing targeted measures to prevent and not just react, through sensitive and accurate communication.
