Should the de-extinction of the dodo be legal?

By Brandie Tang · Age 16 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 24th March 2026

Nowadays, the resurrection of extinct species is no longer an impossible feat; it is very much achievable with modern science and technology. Scientists are currently researching species closest to those extinct with hopes of bringing them back to our ecosystem through genetic engineering. An example of this would be the study of the Nicobar pigeon’s genome, which acts as a template for the dodo bird’s research. Although many are overjoyed with the idea of reviving species from the dead and seeing them thrive in the wild once again, there are many ethical and biological conflicts of this proposition. In this essay, I will be evaluating whether or not the de-extinction of dodo birds should be made legal.

One of the main arguments for resurrecting the dodo specifically, is their likelihood to have a positive influence on the ecosystem. This can be proven through studies of the dodo bird’s fruit-eating diet, which meant that they played a crucial role in dispersing seeds on the island and the growth of the plants there. Additional evidence also suggests that the dodo may have been the only species whose beaks had the capacity to swallow the large seeds of native fruits. Thus following the dodo bird’s extinction, many local plants were threatened with extinction, which threatened the ecology of the island as a whole. Therefore, although the sudden appearance of a species may have a risky effect on the ecological balance in a modern environment, scientists have reason to suspect that dodo birds will not only increase biodiversity, but also help maintain stability in ecosystems.

On Mauritius Island where the dodo thrived, there were numerous cauliflorous plants, making it an ideal environment for a flightless bird. Other than an island of finite resources, there were other factors that contributed to the dodo bird’s docile nature, such as a lack of predators. Unfortunately this made them susceptible to human attacks, which eventually led to their extinction. As such, many believe that it is our moral obligation and responsibility to bring back species that perished because of human activity.

On the other hand, others believe that bringing the extinct back is not viable as compensation to the dodo without a suitable habitat. One of the greatest issues in reviving species lost decades ago, is finding the right environment for them to survive in, reducing the probability of a second extinction. Although raising the dodo in sanctuaries or zoos will protect them from predators or a lack of resources, it may hinder their abilities to defend themselves in the wild, which would lower the possibility of their survival once released. Albeit, raising the dodo in such an environment will allow us to study their behaviour, which will help boost conservation research. This may allow us to further prevent similar species from going extinct, whilst nurturing endangered ones.

Lastly, experts have emphasised their concerns of how de-extinction can raise a false sense of security towards providing assurance that whole species can overcome death, thereby affecting how we view the value of life. This may lead to the attention and funds directed to protecting endangered animals being diverted to reviving dead species, without any guarantee that they can be bought back. Furthermore, this could impact the treatment of existing organisms as a whole. I believe this could lead to an increase in hunting, or decrease in hunting regulations of certain species. Ironically, this would mean that more species would go extinct because of the research towards de-extinction.

To conclude, I believe that the de-extinction of dodo birds should not yet be legal. In my opinion, there has not been enough research done towards a solid foundation for the dodo to survive in. I think that factors such as shelter, terrain, dietary needs, and new ecology should also be considered more carefully, as much has changed over the course of decades. However, if the survival and renewal of their species as a whole is guaranteed with the development of research, their de-extinction should be made legal to support their incorporation into modern ecosystems.
