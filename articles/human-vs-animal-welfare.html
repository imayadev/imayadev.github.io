Is human welfare more important than animal welfare?

By Stella Odysseos · Age 17 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 24th March 2026

Since the appearance of homo sapiens around 300,000 years ago, value has been placed on human life beyond our own survival. Over time, complex social structures developed and humans realised they could create and use tools that not only gave them control over their environment, but also animals. This was a turning point in history as it gave one species power over all others, and the ability to place our needs and welfare above theirs. However, you could argue that just because we can and have placed our welfare above all others, do we have a right to?

One argument for human welfare being raised above animal welfare is that humans have a greater capacity for compassion and complex emotion.This is evidenced by discoveries such as the remains of an elderly homo-erectus jawbone, Dmaninsi Hominin, who lost most of his teeth years before he died. This suggests he was kept alive by the care and compassion of those around him who may have chewed the food for him or found soft food. However, the idea that human welfare is more important than an animal’s because we have a supposedly ‘deeper’ experience of reality is questioned by many experts in animal related fields. For example, Dr James C.Harris at John Hopkins University described empathy as ‘an evolutionary mechanism to maintain social cohesion’, suggesting that all animals part of a social group rely on compassion for survival, whether they are human or not. This is illustrated by observations in nature all the time, even beyond domesticated species. For example, when a group of guides witnessed a baby elephant get caught in a snare, the other elephants in the herd rushed to its aid, all helping to free it without causing any harm. Even more incredible, when the veterinary team went to investigate, the mother elephant did not charge as you might expect, but instead observed and held back; it was as if she understood they were trying to help. In conclusion, not only do animals evidently demonstrate empathy for one another, they appear to show mercy and understanding towards humans also. This is also seen in animals like dogs, monkeys, octopuses and countless others who seem to have rich existences.This can be seen in animal relationships with humans as well, as seen in the love between owners and pets, ecologists, veterinarians, animal biologists and many more. Understanding that animals are not necessarily intellectually or emotionally inferior is difficult as some of the largest industries (agriculture and entertainment) are built on that sentiment, which allows them to disregard animal welfare without feeling remorse.

Another argument in support of animal welfare is that every animal is essential to the ecosystem. To illustrate, Dr Peter Dobias argued that ‘from nature’s point of view, the value of human and animal life is undeniably equal’. This is supported by the fact that although humans can create and think more complexly than animals, this is balanced by humans ability to destroy on a greater scale, and a seeming inability to live in harmony with the environment as animals do. For example, human activity (including industrial pollution, habitat destruction and how we farm) has led to one million species being at risk of extinction globally and an increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide 100 times faster than any natural increase seen in history. Once you step away from a human-centric view, human self-importance seems to stem from our own need to feel superior in an ever-changing and dangerous world, rather than any positive impact we’ve had. Moreover, the only way for humans to thrive is if they start seeing the importance of every animal on our planet and in our lives.

Overall, the reason human welfare is considered more important than animal welfare is because it is what a large proportion of our society and culture has accepted as truth. Upon closer inspection, there is not much evidence that human life is worth more than that of any animal, other than our own sense of species superiority.
