To what extent does current politics influence the ethical concept of patient choice in the NHS?

By Tiana Majumder · Age 16 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 21st February 2026

“No decision about me, without me” is a slogan that captures the government’s vision of greater choice in the NHS. It commands respect, equality, and a rejection of paternalism, placing patient choice at the centre of care. While this symbolic understanding of choice is empowering, its appeal makes it vulnerable to political distortion. When such appeals are used to legitimise harmful health behaviours, such as the anti-vaccination movement, the ethical value of patient choice is compromised, raising questions about when it benefits the NHS and how it should be integrated in practice.

The NHS Constitution strives to expand the patient choice agenda, increasingly enabling patients to select providers, hospital locations, and modes of care. But why does choice matter beyond ticking boxes for autonomy or beneficence? Autonomy gives patients control, and beneficence recognises their ability to judge what is in their best interests to make a choice. However, as argued by philosopher Scanlon, the moral power of choice lies not in the act of choosing itself but in the opportunity to choose: to be denied this opportunity can signal inferiority and question the competency of patients.

Why do patients continue to value choice even when they do not wish to decide? Even when patients defer to clinicians they trust to act in their best interest to make a decision, the fact that they were offered the choice recognises their competence and reduces the power imbalance within the doctor-patient relationship. Choosing not to decide is itself a choice, and is not inherently a failure to respect their status. Respecting patients’ symbolic choice may require clinicians to act against their own view of what is beneficent for the patient, but only within limits that avoid harming patient safety. Crucially, this does not reduce clinicians to compliant information providers: they must exercise judgement while ensuring patient inclusivity in decisions that affect them.

Fully autonomous or not, the opportunity of choice also preserves equity in healthcare for vulnerable or marginalised patients and reduces the risk of unequal treatment. In this way, the symbolic value of patient choice takes into account the nuance of the decision-making process, making it a particularly beneficial framework for the delivery of healthcare in the NHS.

Yet, the ethical appeal of choice makes it a powerful weapon in politics. “Choice” has become a politically attractive term, and is ubiquitous in government policies. As David Cameron’s comments on expanding MMR availability illustrate, politicians are acutely aware of the popularity of choice and the electoral risk of limiting it. In such contexts, choice risks becoming performative, often leveraged for popularity and voter appeal rather than patient empowerment.

This tension becomes more apparent in the context of vaccination policy. Appeals to autonomy, framed as “our children, our choice”, have been used to legitimise vaccine refusal despite clear public health harms. While refusal may be ethically acceptable in exceptional cases, misinformation and politicised narratives threaten herd immunity. An analysis of 500,000 social media posts shows that vaccine sceptics prioritise liberty and freedom of choice, whereas advocates emphasise care and collective protection. In the NHS, this reveals a key risk of the patient choice agenda: when choice is promoted as an unquestioned good, it can be misused in ways that undermine public health.

Patient choice is more than just a slogan or an agenda; it empowers us in our healthcare. Yet when choice is exploited or misunderstood politically, it can imperil both individual and public health, as the anti-vaccination movement starkly illustrates. Moving forward, clinicians must educate, support and build trust. The patient, not the policy, the politics, or the disease, must remain at the centre of care, ensuring that every decision is truly made with patients for their benefit.
