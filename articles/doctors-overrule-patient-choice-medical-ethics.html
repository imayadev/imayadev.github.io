Should doctors ever overrule a patient's choices?

By Toni Fafowora · Age 17 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 8th March 2026

The question of whether doctors should ever overrule a patient’s choices sits at the core of medical ethics. It requires balancing two fundamental principles: autonomy, and beneficence.The word autonomy is composed of the Greek word ‘autos’, meaning self, and ‘nomos’, meaning rule. Initially being referred to the self rule of independent cities, this has since been extended to mean individual self governance. Beneficence is the ethical duty of healthcare professionals to act in the best interest of their patients, actively promoting their well-being, preventing harm, and providing benefit through compassionate, patient-centered care. Autonomy and beneficence unequivocally play essential roles within medicine. Being one of the four pillars of medical ethics, patient autonomy is a central principle,grounded in the belief that all competent adults with capacity have the right to make informed decisions about their own bodies. It is a core pillar that requires healthcare providers to obtain informed consent from patients before any treatment or procedure. Likewise, beneficence is crucial in medicine as it's the ethical duty to actively promote patient well-being, and prevent harm, Yet,patients may refuse lifesaving treatment, make choices that appear irrational or even be impaired by mental illness. . In such circumstances, doctors face a dichotomy between respecting autonomy and fulfilling their professional and legal obligation to act in the patient’s best interests.

The philosophical underpinning for autonomy, as interpreted by philosophers Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), the father of the idea that humans are rational, autonomous agents with intrinsic dignity, and John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), a foundational figure in the philosophical basis of patient autonomy, is that all persons have intrinsic and unconditional worth, and therefore, should have the power to make rational decisions and moral choices, and each should be allowed to exercise his or her capacity for self-determination.According to the principles of biomedical ethics developed by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, doctors should always respect patient autonomy as a general moral rule. Their framework posits that autonomy is one of four foundational principles that should guide ethical decision-making in healthcare. While autonomy is a core principle, it must be balanced with the other three principles: beneficence (acting for the patient's good), nonmaleficence (doing no harm), and justice (fair distribution of resources).Two conditions are ordinarily required before a decision can be regarded as autonomous. The individual has to have the relevant internal capacities for self-government and has to be free from external constraints. In a medical context, a decision is ordinarily regarded as autonomous where the individual has the capacity to make the relevant decision, has sufficient information to make the decision and does so voluntarily..When a patient lacks mental capacity, decisions are typically made based on their best interests. For example, if a severely confused diabetic were to refuse insulin, doctors have an obligation to intervene to act in the patient's best interest and administer treatment. Additionally, decisions may be overruled if it puts the lives of others out of harm. For example, if a patient with tuberculosis were to refuse isolation, doctors and public health officials in the UK can take a series of escalating steps, starting with persuasion and support and potentially leading to mandatory isolation enforced by legal orders as public safety always overrides.

There have been various cases where a patient's decision has been overruled to act in their best interest. In 1997, MB, a pregnant woman needed a C-section to safely deliver her baby, and yet she refused as she had a severe needle phobia and panicked. However, The court ruled that although she was normally competent, the panic attacks temporarily robbed her of capacity. Doctors were justified in overriding her refusal to ensure her and her baby’s safety. Furthermore, In the case of In re E.G. , a 17-year-old Jehovah’s Witness girl with leukemia refused blood transfusions that were medically essential because of her personal beliefs.. While the court acknowledged that she was a mature and thoughtful young woman, it also considered the fact that she was under the age of legal adulthood and facing a completely preventable death.The court ultimately allowed doctors to overrule her refusal and administer the transfusion. Many similar cases have occurred when minors refuse chemotherapy, surgeries, or vaccinations. In such situations, courts consistently prioritize preserving the child’s life over honoring their stated wishes, arguing that minors do not yet possess full legal autonomy.For example, Re E (Blood Transfusion) (2021): The court overrode the refusal of a blood transfusion by a 15-year-old Jehovah's Witness, even though he was considered Gillick competent in many respects. The court's primary duty to preserve life and act in the child's best interest took precedence due to the grave and real threat to his life.

In conclusion, I would say that doctors should almost always respect autonomy whenever the patient has the capacity to understand their situation, weigh the consequences, and make a voluntary and informed choice. However, respect for autonomy is not absolute, and there are circumstances in which doctors cannot simply follow a patient’s wishes, such as when the patient lacks capacity, is acting under coercion or extreme distress, or when the decision places a child or the public at serious risk. In these situations override is ethically justified as doctors have an ethical and legal duty to prevent harm and act in the patient’s best interests Therefore, while patient decisions should be respected in the vast majority of cases, they should not always be followed blindly.
