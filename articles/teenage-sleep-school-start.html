Teenage sleep - why UK secondary school students should start school later in the day according to science

By Ourania Neophytou · Age 18 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 6th March 2026

In the 21st century, society increasingly fails to prioritize sleep as an integral pillar of health, many times as a result of standardised routines, enforced to maximise a population with all its constituent individuals awake in the same hours in order for easier cooperation and socialisation. While this may sound like a utopian paradise, it often results in disastrous knock-on effects for our sleep, because our bodies do not naturally follow the same routine pattern of sleep and wakefulness, even within the same lifetime. This can have serious consequences for our health, especially in secondary school children.

An individual’s chronotype is their natural inclination to be awake or asleep, regarding the time of day. You may unknowingly have come across this concept already, via the colloquial phrase ‘morning lark or night owl’. In reality, there are more than just 2 chronotypes- they exist on a spectrum, with no two individuals experiencing the same 24 hour rhythm. Chronotypes are biologically determined by 2 main factors:

Firstly, your circadian rhythm. Your ‘body clock’ (roughly 24 hours) is generated within the brain and communicated to every cell in the body, influencing many routine processes such as hormone levels, body temperature and metabolism. The actual ‘clock’ structure is the suprachiasmatic nucleus, found at the crossing point of the optic nerves in the brain, where it ‘samples’ the light levels detected by the eyes. Light exposure, food, and even social interactions can all modify this natural rhythm, which is handy for getting over jet lag on holiday, but inconvenient considering the unnatural amounts of light exposure we get in the evenings from various devices.

Secondly, your genetic code, otherwise known as your DNA. In simple terms, differences in what the DNA in your body actually codes for and how active or ‘turned on’ that code is predetermines a lot of our chronotypes. The code for this does not come from a single gene (section of DNA), but it includes a section called the period gene. In fruit flies, this gene was researched by the winners of the 2017 Nobel Prize for Medicine. They discovered that this particular section coded for the production of a protein that fluctuates in abundance throughout the day, forming a biochemical cycle across a 24 hour period. This happens in every cell in humans as well, and creates a biological ‘clock’ in every part of our bodies.

The human circadian rhythm shifts around 3 hours forward from the ages of 10 to 18. The average adolescent starts to feel tired at around 11.00pm, compared to around 9.30 pm in adults, and needs about 9 hours of sleep. Unfortunately, this is impossible to follow for the majority of 11-18 year olds across the country, as most UK secondary schools start between 8 am and 9 am. This favours individuals with naturally earlier chronotypes and robs the average adolescent of around a quarter of the sleep they need, causing a number of adverse effects.

Many studies show the link between sleep loss and cardiac failure. In a Japanese study with over 2,000 participants, six hours a night for 14 years was associated with triple the risk of coronary events than for those sleeping 7-7.9 hours. Another study found even acute sleep loss on one night promptly raises heart rate and blood pressure, both intensely damaging long term. Less sleep increases ghrelin levels (signals hunger), and decreases leptin levels (signals satiation): the less you sleep, the more you eat, increasing risk for chronic illnesses like diabetes and obesity. All bodily systems are harmed by sleep loss.

Unfortunately, the brain is also severely impacted by sleep loss. Sleep deprived individuals react more to negative emotional triggers than those who sleep a full night. In one study, the amygdala (associated with anger and aggression) was 60% more active when sleep deprived. The striatum (associated with impulsivity) was also hyperactive. Loss of REM sleep in particular is linked to emotional instability, as REM includes dreams, which reprocess emotional memories, especially those related to fear,

The less you sleep, the lower your concentration, creativity, and cognitive function. Memories cannot be formed or moved to long term as easily, so the efficiency of education received decreases as information cannot be retained. Unfortunately, this may even deprive students of more sleep as it will take even more time to commit information to memory, or gain the practice they need to improve their aptitude in a subject.

As we can see, the vast majority of teenage students are no longer proverbial early birds, due to inescapable biological processes and not laziness. However, despite the undeniable evidence, school start times continue to prioritise synchronicity over sleep, leaving students to deal with the alarming consequences of a routine that fails to maximise their natural potential and also damages their health day by day. This adds unnecessary strain to the NHS, by deteriorating the health and quality of life of adolescents nationwide in the present and also for the future, into their adult lives. In conclusion, it is my belief that in order to solve these issues, we should leave early start times for secondary schools in the past, and move into an age where a good night’s sleep gets the attention it deserves, starting within the institutions that shape us for the rest of our lives.
