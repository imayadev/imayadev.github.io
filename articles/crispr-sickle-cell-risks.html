Can CRISPR Cures for Sickle Cell Disease Create New Risks in Our Blood?

By Amal Amzath Ali · Age 16 (Senior Category) · JSS International School, Dubai · 23rd March 2026

For people living with sickle cell disease, CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing has been described as nothing short of revolutionary. Instead of treating symptoms over a lifetime, scientists can now edit a patient’s own blood stem cells so they start making healthier red blood cells, sometimes after just a single treatment. But whenever we change the DNA of long‑lived stem cells, it raises a natural question: are we solving one problem while quietly creating another?

Sickle cell disease and why it’s so hard to treat

Sickle cell disease is caused by a single mutation in the gene for beta‑globin, a component of haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in red blood cells. This tiny change causes haemoglobin molecules to stick together, especially when oxygen levels are low, which bends red blood cells into a rigid “sickle” shape. These sickled cells can block small blood vessels, leading to severe pain crises, organ damage, and a shorter life expectancy.

Until recently, treatment mainly focused on managing symptoms: pain relief, blood transfusions, and a few drugs that reduce crises but don’t fully solve the underlying problem. Bone marrow transplants can be curative, but require a matched donor and carry serious risks, so they are not an option for most patients.

How CRISPR and Casgevy change the game

CRISPR-Cas9 offers a very different approach. Instead of replacing the patient’s bone marrow with someone else’s, doctors take the patient’s own hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) out of their body, edit them in the lab, and then return them. These HSCs live in the bone marrow and are responsible for making all the different types of blood cells throughout a person’s life.

The first approved CRISPR therapy for sickle cell disease, called Casgevy, doesn’t directly fix the sickle mutation. Instead, it targets a gene called BCL11A, which normally turns off fetal haemoglobin shortly after birth. Using CRISPR-Cas9, scientists make a precise cut in a regulatory region that BCL11A needs to work in red blood cells. When this region is disrupted, BCL11A can no longer fully shut down fetal haemoglobin, so edited stem cells start producing high levels of this “baby” haemoglobin instead.

Fetal haemoglobin does not sickle, so it acts like a molecular “backup plan”: it dilutes the sickling haemoglobin and keeps red blood cells flexible. In clinical trials, patients receiving Casgevy show strongly reduced pain crises and often become independent of regular blood transfusions, with these benefits lasting for several years of follow‑up so far.

The hidden side of making DNA breaks

CRISPR-Cas9 works by creating a double-strand break (DSB) at a specific point in the DNA. The cell then has to repair this break. The main repair pathway is called non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which is quick but messy. It often introduces small insertions or deletions (“indels”) that change the DNA sequence at the cut site—exactly what we want when our goal is to disrupt a regulatory region like the BCL11A enhancer.

However, double-strand breaks can also cause more complex problems. Sometimes the repair process leads to large deletions, inversions (where a DNA segment flips), or other structural changes in the genome near the cut site. These are not always easy to detect with standard short-read sequencing methods that mainly focus on small changes.

On top of that, DNA breaks activate a stress response in the cell, often involving the famous tumour suppressor protein p53. If the damage is too severe, p53 can push the cell toward self-destruction, which is good for preventing cancer but also creates a strong selection pressure. In a population of edited stem cells, any clone that happens to have changes in DNA repair genes or the p53 pathway might survive and divide better than its neighbours.

What is clonal hematopoiesis, and why does it matter?

This is where clonal hematopoiesis comes in. Clonal hematopoiesis is a state where a single mutant hematopoietic stem cell clone gradually expands and takes up a noticeable share of blood production, even in people who otherwise appear healthy. These clones often carry mutations in genes like DNMT3A, TET2, TP53, or PPM1D, which are also found in blood cancers.

By itself, clonal hematopoiesis does not mean someone has leukaemia. But studies have shown that people with these clones have a higher risk of developing myeloid malignancies (such as myelodysplastic syndrome or acute myeloid leukaemia) later in life. They may also have increased cardiovascular risk, possibly because mutant immune cells promote inflammation.

We already know that certain stresses can encourage these clones to grow. For example, chemotherapy and radiation—both of which cause DNA damage—can give an advantage to stem cells with mutations that make them more resistant to damage, like those in TP53 or PPM1D. Over time, this can shift the balance of the blood system toward a few dominant mutant clones.

Could CRISPR editing in sickle cell disease promote risky clones?

My hypothesis builds on this idea: CRISPR-Cas9 editing in sickle cell disease might, in combination with other stresses, create conditions that favor the expansion of certain edited stem cell clones. The treatment already includes several potential sources of stress on hematopoietic stem cells.

First, the editing itself introduces double-strand breaks in long‑lived stem cells and relies on error‑prone repair. A subset of cells will carry complex structural variants or DNA changes near the target site, and some may gain subtle advantages in survival or growth. Second, patients typically undergo myeloablative conditioning—strong chemotherapy that clears space in the bone marrow before the edited cells are reinfused. This kind of conditioning is known from transplant studies to introduce DNA damage and can contribute to late‑appearing blood cancers in some patients.

Finally, sickle cell disease itself creates a chronically inflamed bone marrow environment, with ongoing tissue damage and repair. Inflammatory signals can influence which stem cell clones thrive or struggle. Put together, CRISPR editing, harsh conditioning, and chronic inflammation might form a “perfect storm” where a small subset of edited clones gradually dominates the blood system over many years.

So far, clinical trials of Casgevy have not reported therapy-related leukaemias or clear signs of dangerous clonal hematopoiesis, which is very reassuring. But follow‑up is still relatively short—measured in years, not decades—and the number of treated patients is still modest compared with how many people might eventually receive this therapy. More sensitive sequencing methods and long‑term tracking of variant allele frequencies in blood cells will be needed to rule out rare, late-emerging problems.

Looking ahead: powerful cures, careful monitoring

None of this means we should step back from CRISPR-based cures for sickle cell disease. For patients who have lived with severe pain crises, organ damage, and limited treatment options, therapies like Casgevy offer life‑changing benefits and, for many, a realistic chance at a functional cure. Instead, it suggests that our excitement should go hand‑in‑hand with caution and careful long-term follow‑up.

In the future, next‑generation editing tools such as base editors and prime editors may help reduce these risks by avoiding double-strand breaks altogether and making more precise changes to DNA. At the same time, integrating regular genomic monitoring into follow‑up care for gene‑edited patients could help us catch any unusual clonal expansions early, long before they turn into serious disease.

CRISPR therapies for sickle cell disease show how fast biology is moving from the lab to the clinic. The challenge for our generation of scientists and doctors will be not only to invent powerful new cures, but also to understand and manage their subtle long‑term consequences in the most careful way possible.
