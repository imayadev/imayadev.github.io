Revolutionary fentanyl vaccine undertakes a 2026 human trial

By Kate Ip · Age 15 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 26th March 2026

ARMR Sciences has been developing a new anti-fentanyl vaccine that has recently entered a human trial in early 2026. This is a novel immunotherapy vaccine that produces fentanyl-binding antibodies which neutralise this synthetic opioid in the bloodstream and prevent it from reaching the brain. If approved widely, it could be used to save tens of thousands of lives from fatal overdoses and accidental fentanyl poisoning.

What is fentanyl?

Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid that is 35-50 times more potent than heroin and 100 times stronger than morphine; it binds to mu-opioid receptors in the brain and the spinal cord, which when activated by opioids, can lead to the inhibition of pain signalling and can cause a euphoric high. Additionally, it inhibits the brain’s control of breathing, so respiratory depression is the primary cause of fatal fentanyl overdose. A 2 milligram dose of fentanyl - similar to a few grains of sand - can be fatal, so this new development is a way to prevent fentanyl-related fatalities. The opioid also requires multiple or stronger doses of naloxone (Narcan) to reverse its effects by attaching to opioid receptors and displacing the fentanyl molecules.

How the vaccine works

Fentanyl molecules are so fatal because they are much smaller than pathogens so the immune system doesn’t react to it, which leads to potential overdoses. However, ARMR has tied the fentanyl molecules to a deactivated diphtheria toxin (CRM197) and an E. coli toxin (dmLT), both of which are already used in vaccines in the market and aren’t toxic in this modified form; they are bound to a synthetic piece of fentanyl which doesn’t cause side effects.

When the immune system realises the presence of this fentanyl compound, it triggers a production of antibodies which bind to fentanyl, which sequesters it from invading the blood-brain barrier (a protective membrane which protects the brain from pathogens and toxins). This is because the fentanyl molecules are now too large to pass through due to it being bound to the fentanyl antibodies. Usually the euphoric highs and respiratory depressions are caused because the molecules are small enough to pass straight through the membrane, attaching themselves to the opioid receptors, but since they are now too large, the antibody-bound fentanyl molecules are passed out of the body as waste through urine.

Results from preclinical testing

In this novel vaccine, testing on rats has shown a 98% block of fentanyl from reaching the brain and up to 100% “elimination of addictive behavioural effects”, Collin Gage (co-founder and CEO of ARMR) said. It has yielded incredible results in their preclinical trials and has great promise. The effect of this vaccine on rats lasted 20 weeks, but because rats only live a few years, it is predicted to be an equivalent of around 1-2 years in humans. Testing has shown that immunisation using the anti-fentanyl vaccine can protect the rats from fatal overdoses. These results suggest that continuous, active vaccination could be a preventative measure to prevent fentanyl fatalities. However, fentanyl is much more potent in humans than in rats, where human tolerance is around 0.029 mg/kg (the assumption of a 70kg human) whereas for rats it's about 4 mg/kg. This creates the question of whether this vaccine could be effectively administered in humans for the prevention of fentanyl effects and overdoses in comparison to the rats.

In further animal studies, no cross-reactivity was found with other opioid-based pharmaceuticals - painkillers and opioid abuse treatments, such as morphine, oxycodone, methadone, and buprenorphine (partial-opioid agonist that reduces cravings and withdrawal symptoms). Another treatment is naltrexone - an opioid receptor antagonist - which, unlike buprenorphine, isn’t opioid-based, but still binds to opioid receptors and reduces cravings and the euphoric high from the drug. Currently, ARMR is testing their vaccine in combination with naltrexone to improve the reduction of fentanyl effects and dangers.

Early 2026 human trials

Scheduled to begin in early 2026, ARMR’s Phase 1 trial will enrol 40 healthy adults to assess the safety of the vaccine and to determine the optimum dosage of the drug. Two shots of varying doses will be introduced intravenously to the volunteers and samples of their blood will be taken to measure blood antibody levels to ensure the vaccine is capable of triggering the production of anti-fentanyl antibodies.

Phase 2 of the trial, if Phase 1 goes well, tests for efficacy. Medical dosages of fentanyl, used for pain relief in medical procedures, will be administered to a small group of participants to assess how well the vaccine works to block the effects of fentanyl. The participants will be closely monitored and under the supervision of experienced anaesthetists. The two phases will be in the Netherlands where the centre has experience in performing studies on medications that reverse opioid effects, such as naloxone (Narcan).

A potential problem

Some raise the question of whether a large enough dose of fentanyl could bypass the body’s supply of anti-fentanyl antibodies to make its way through the blood-brain barrier and it is a plausible concern, confirmed one of ARMR’s medical advisors. This is an even bigger concern with many street drugs, such as heroin, being laced with fentanyl due to its potency, low cost, and ease of production. This means people could accidentally overdose on fentanyl without even knowing they were taking it. Even if immunised by taking the fentanyl vaccine, overdosing could still be possible.

The market for fentanyl vaccines

ARMR developed this vaccine initially with funding from the US Department of Defense due to increasing fentanyl addiction in the US. There is a concern of fentanyl being used as a chemical weapon because of its potency, but by developing this incredible vaccine, it is more than possible to save tens of thousands of lives from either it being used as a weapon, or from overdosing.

In 2018 in the US alone, more than 46,000 people died of opioid overdose, more than 67% of it due to synthetic opioid, with fentanyl contributing massively. If communities that were at higher risk of overdosing on fentanyl were vaccinated, such as younger groups, more people would be motivated to break the addiction from fentanyl. Combined with therapy or group therapy sessions with other addicts, this vaccine could be a remarkable step to combating fentanyl, and other opioid addictions.
