Who Really Benefits From Urban Planning?

By Belle Fung · Age 16 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 24th March 2026

On the surface level, people often perceive urban planning as a technical process that is aimed at optimising land use, resources, and infrastructure to achieve maximum efficiency and economic growth - a process that is viewed as neutral and value-free. In reality, however, urban planning decisions are deeply embedded with moral values that reflect society's priorities, shaping who benefits and who bears the costs of development - whether this is the environment, people, or the economy.

Historically, urban planners have aimed to deliver "optimal" spatial solutions which are often closely tied with economic competitiveness and growth, through methods such as zoning, which is widely used in England. In London, zoning has concentrated commercial and financial activity within areas such as the Central Activities Zone, widely regarded as the UK's economic powerhouse. While this approach can be framed as locally efficient and economically successful, it prioritises the economic output of Central London at the expense of the social wellbeing of people that live in outlying areas. Outer zones have experienced an increase in deprivation and dereliction as a result of this zoning strategy, all whilst Central continues to prosper. This demonstrates that such "optimal" solutions are not neutral technical outcomes, but reflect underlying value judgments, where the benefits enjoyed by some are prioritised above the social and economic costs borne by others.

The proposed Heathrow expansion further illustrates this moral complexity within urban planning. Heathrow is the UK's biggest airport and an important driver of jobs and revenue, supporting over 220,000 workers. Supporters of this expansion argue that expanding the airport is crucial for London to maintain its position as a leading global hub, and also to keep up with competitors like New York and Paris. In this sense, economic growth and global connectivity are viewed as essential goals for the country's prosperity. Yet, these economic benefits would come with considerable social and environmental costs. Heathrow is already the largest source of CO: emissions in the UK, and the construction of a third runway would significantly exacerbate this impact, directly conflicting with national and global climate targets. The environmental implications of such planning decisions have become increasingly significant in recent years, particularly in light of accelerating global warming and its visible consequences. As a result, planners are now under greater pressure to incorporate sustainable building practices and environmental considerations, reflecting a growing shift in planning priorities toward long-term environmental responsibility.

This example clearly helps to demonstrate that urban planning is not merely a technical exercise, but with ethical practice being an inseparable part of it. Typically, it shows that when economic growth and global competitiveness are narrowly privileged, planning can intensify inequality by marginalising vulnerable communities and exacerbating environmental harm on a global scale. This tension highlights the dual nature of planning as both a technical and moral activity, proving that these decisions are never neutral or purely objective - they concretize society's priorities, making visible whose interests are served and whose are sacrificed.

Conversely, urban planning can also operate as a powerful instrument of equity and empowerment when it is guided by explicit ethical commitments rather than pure economic objectives. By deliberately prioritising social inclusion and environmental sustainability, urban planning has the capacity to challenge existing local inequalities and reshape urban environments in more sustainable ways. In this context, planning becomes a mechanism for redistributing resources, improving access to essential services, and protecting marginalised communities such as those suffering from poverty.

Cities such as Copenhagen offer a compelling example of how ethical priorities can be embedded within planning practice. Through sustained investment in cycling infrastructure, pedestrian-friendly streets, and low-emission zones, Copenhagen has positioned environmental responsibility and public health as central planning values. These strategies reflect broader societal values that prioritise long-term climate responsibility and collective social wellbeing over short-term economic gain. Yet, most importantly, Copenhagen demonstrates that environmental sustainability and economic vitality can coexist, offering a practical model of planning that aligns ethical priorities with effective urban development.

In conclusion, urban planning decisions are far from being purely technical or value-free, where its moral priorities are shaped by the intentions it seeks to fulfill. The proposed Heathrow expansion demonstrates how prioritising economic growth and global influence can come at the expense of human wellbeing and environmental sustainability, reflecting societal values that often favour competitiveness over care. At the same time, planning can deliberately prioritise social and environmental wellbeing to promote equity and inclusion. By recognising and addressing the tension between social, economic, and environmental priorities, planners can guide urban development toward outcomes that are more just, sustainable, and beneficial for all members of society.
