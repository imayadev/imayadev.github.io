How humans are trying to build a city on the Moon!

By Nuen Wang · Age 16 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 12th February 2026

For the first time in over 50 years, humans are preparing to set foot on the Moon again. NASA’s Artemis III mission, set to launch by 2028, will send four astronauts there and back. At the same time, Elon Musk and SpaceX just unveiled even more ambitious plans for a “self-growing city” to be built on the Moon within the next 10 years! It sounds like science fiction — but is it? Could we really build a city on the Moon? And if so, how?

To answer that, we need to understand what makes living on the Moon so difficult — and how modern science is beginning to solve those problems.

Since Apollo 17 in 1972, despite us not returning to the Moon, humanity has still made enormous leaps in technological advancements: sending over 200 spacecraft across the solar system (like Mars!), and building an International Space Station (The ISS) in low Earth orbit. Getting there is no longer the main challenge, the real question is how can we survive there for longer periods of time?

The lunar surface has extreme temperature variations due to the lack of an atmosphere, ranging from a scorching 127C in daytime to a freezing -173C during the lunar night. But the biggest threats are the mini meteorites (micrometeorites) and deadly cosmic rays from outer space.

Both the Earth and the moon experience a daily shower of small rocks and debris from space, and cancer-inducing cosmic rays from the sun. Luckily on Earth, our thick atmosphere absorbs almost all of these miscellaneous space travellers, allowing us to safely sunbathe outside. Unfortunately the Moon is much different, without any protection from the hazards of space, it is bombarded daily by meteors and cosmic rays, making the surface very dangerous for setting up settlements long term. Around 100 ping-pong ball sized meteors hit the Moon every day, and although small, some travel at over 70km/s, 50 times faster than a bullet! A direct hit could lead to a disaster.

Standing on the surface of the Moon, you would receive ~200x the radiation you would on Earth. After just a couple of months, there would be a significant increase in cancer risks, as these cosmic rays from the sun and outer space penetrate into your body and damage DNA, causing cells to mutate. 500 days on the lunar surface exceeds the total dosage of radiation imposed by NASA on astronauts for their entire career! Thankfully, these rays are not very penetrating, just a few metres of lunar soil (called regolith) overhead would bring down radiation exposure to levels comparable to staying on the ISS (~2.5x Earth surface levels), much safer.

Fortunately, not all parts of the Moon are equally harsh. Closer to the poles, the temperature is much more manageable although still quite chilly: ranging from -50C - 0C. Due to the nature of the Moon’s axial tilt, similar to the long Antarctica summers on Earth, the southern pole of the Moon receives near constant sunlight, with the sun staying above the horizon for 90% of the lunar year in some areas. Perfect for reliable solar power!

Despite how it looks now, the Moon used to be a ball of hot lava when it first formed, and it's full of lava tunnels carved by ancient lava flows, similar to caves formed by flowing water on Earth. These lunar “caves” are perfect for both shielding lunar citizens from the intense solar radiation and further reducing surface temperature fluctuations. The sun never reaches down into the deepest parts of these caves, and this results in some of the coldest places in the solar system, at a bone-chilling -230C, we can use these deep caves as storage spaces for ice, which can be melted into usable water.

Water is actually pretty easy to come by, probes such as the Chandrayaan-1, launched in 2008, detected pockets of ice in many of the deep, permanently shadowed craters on the moon, by extracting and purifying it we can use it for much more than drinking. Through electrolysis, water (H20) can be split into its two elements, oxygen (for breathing) and hydrogen, rocket fuel! Mixing pure liquid hydrogen with oxygen is actually one of the most effective ways to create rocket fuel. Not only does lunar ice offer an easy source of drinking water and breathable oxygen, it could also be used to make the rocket fuel needed for when astronauts return to Earth – making a Moon base far more self-sufficient.

So: a sunny region near the southern lunar pole, with over 70% sunshine year round, and access to nearby underground lava tunnels for water storage and shielding from cosmic rays, our first lunar settlers have found an ideal place to settle down.

Now, our ground crew have a source of power, a place to build a sheltered home and access to water, we just need to feed them.

Growing food however, is another problem. Plants can't be grown in the lunar soil, it is far too nutrient poor and toxic. Soil from Earth is heavy and would be extremely costly to transport over in large amounts, so instead, we can use aquaponics! An aquaponic is a setup for growing plants and fish together, in a closed loop, where the fish poop fertilizes the water that the plants grow in and the plants filter and clean that water, recycling fresh water back into the fish tank, and it all works without any soil! Still it requires regular feeding of the fish to keep the system running, but it allows us to grow fish and plants together in a compact area, saving space and providing moon settlers with a more nutritious and varied diet. Ongoing research projects such as the French based “Lunar Hatch” are actively testing methods of setting up aquaponics which could work in zero/low gravity such as on the moon.

But of course, there's still many more challenges our brave astronauts need to overcome. The Moon has only 1/6th of the gravity as we do Earth, and whilst it would be fun for the first few weeks, long term stays in low gravity has big repercussions on the human body including muscle wasting and loss in bone density. Currently, astronauts working in zero gravity conditions such as onboard the ISS have to exercise very regularly to combat and slow down this process. But in the future, moon habitats could incorporate centrifugal designs; recreating the sensation of gravity by spinning the module at high speeds, but until then, working out a lot is still mandatory.

Finally, we know it can be done, but the bigger question is, how much will it cost? Current projections from NASA and the private sector estimate ~$20 - $40 billion. For comparison, the International Space Station has cost ~$150 billion over its lifetime. So, it's not really that big of an investment for us, considering the possible benefits could be huge! A Moon base is the first step towards interplanetary and possibly even interstellar colonization. Going to mars would probably mean stopping by the moon first. The scientific and technological advancements would benefit all of mankind and maybe even give us a motive to finally unite and resolve our conflicts down here on Earth, now that we’re really an interplanetary species.

So, wishing the best of luck to those first moon pioneers, and maybe one day, we’ll be able to wave up at the Moon and actually get a response back!
