Live Aid - a Milestone for Satellite Television

By Kerry Amoils · Age 17 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 25th March 2026

For many, Live Aid sparks images of Freddie Mercury with a captivated crowd at his fingertips, or of a grand line up of 1980s stars united on stage for the finale ‘Do They Know it’s Christmas’. However, the technological accomplishments of the renowned charity concert are sometimes overlooked. Broadcasting the event to 1.9bn people across 150 countries (a viewing figure made up of nearly 40% of the world’s population!) was demanding on equipment of the time. The ability to view the ‘super concert’ in more remote regions, including parts of the Soviet Union, was due to the use of satellite television. Live Aid was the most ambitious international satellite TV event ever staged at the time - so how are performances able to reach our homes?

A television signal’s journey begins at a ground station on Earth, within an antenna. This location was Philadelphia, in the field behind JFK stadium which had been colonised entirely by satellite dishes and trailers. An alternating current is applied to the antenna, causing electrons in the metal to accelerate back and forth. These oscillations generate magnetic fields and electric fields perpendicular to them, which will ‘detach’ from the antenna as electromagnetic waves; radio waves. Information can be encoded into the radio waves through either amplitude modulation or more commonly, frequency modulation - hence the term ‘FM radio’.

We now have our uplink signal, heading towards a geostationary satellite. These satellites are in geostationary orbit, a path 35800km in altitude above the Earth. The orbit is sometimes referred to as the ‘Clarke orbit’, and the satellites along it as the ‘Clarke belt’ after Arthur C Clarke who popularised the concept in the Wireless World Magazine (1945). It is at this distance that the satellite’s orbital period matches that of the Earth, so they remain stationary in comparison to the antennas on Earth. These satellites are also equipped with small engines known as thrusters, producing small amounts of force to keep the satellite in the correct orientation, countering the gravitational pull of both the sun and moon. The satellite transponders receive the uplink at a certain frequency and amplify them. The downlink signal is transmitted at a lower frequency back to Earth, over a broader ‘footprint’ area. To broadcast the performances from both locations to viewers worldwide, Live Aid employed a groundbreaking 13 satellites, 5 of these operated by Intelsat who still deliver channels to approximately 2.3bn people globally.

Satellite dishes on the ground are placed to receive the downlink signal. The dishes are parabolic antennas, a shape chosen not just for aesthetic purposes but for its ability to reflect all detected signals towards a specific focal point. As the signal picked up by the dishes is very weak, it must be amplified by a low noise block downconverter. This is a type of receiver which acts as:

  • A frequency mixer - a circuit that outputs frequencies differing from the input frequencies.
  • A local oscillator - a circuit used to produce alternating current.

These circuits work in conjunction with one another as a converter, while adding as little noise to the signal as possible. Minimising the additional noise was vital to the effective broadcast of Live Aid - how else would one have been able to appreciate the fine vocals and the powerful violin solo of Ultravox’s ‘Vienna’? Now, the signal can be sent through the focal point of the parabola (the feedhorn) and further through the coaxial cable to home satellite receivers. Here, the signal will be decoded as specific data from the broader transmission is selected.

It is due to these satellite communications that we are still able to enjoy live concerts from the leisure of our own living rooms, with seemingly no isolation from the in- person event. However, the sheer distance which the signal must travel across can provide some obstacles, particularly to those as ambitious as the stars of Live Aid. Electromagnetic waves have the endlessly useful capability of travelling at the speed of light; nothing else is known to exceed these parameters. Although, the distance crossed is vast enough to result in a delay of seconds between the real occasion and the experience behind the television screen. For instance, synchronisation problems prevented David Bowie and Mick Jagger from performing a transatlantic duet.

It was not the satellite link up alone which highlighted the technological expertise allowing Live Aid to function. The concert was a showcase of all forms of achievements within engineering. It could not have run so seamlessly without the civil engineers who designed the revolving stage so different bands could set up and perform simultaneously, the sound engineers upholding the quality of each act, and even the mechanical engineers who designed the Concorde taken by Phil Collins, fulfilling his aim to perform in both the US and UK venues on the day. This is just one example to prove that neither the Arts nor STEM sit on a pedestal compared to one another. Both industries work hand in hand to provide us with top quality entertainment; events treasured for years to come.
