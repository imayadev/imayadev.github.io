Covid: The mRNA Vaccine Story

By Charisse Cheung · Age 16 (Senior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 27th March 2026

23rd March, 2026, marks the 6th anniversary of the first Covid lockdown in the UK. At the time, Covid was still in its beginning stages, having just been declared a global pandemic by the World Health Organisation due to its highly contagious nature. It could easily be transmitted from person to person, allowing the disease to rapidly spread across the world since the first recorded case in China in December 2019. At its peak, there were over confirmed 100,000 cases per day, with many more going unreported due to lack of symptoms.

The UK took the pandemic in stride, becoming the first country to launch a national Covid-19 vaccination programme on the 8th of December, 2020, with the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine. It first focused on protecting vulnerable elders, key workers, and those who are immunocompromised (clinically more susceptible to illness). Within basically a year of the first case of Covid, a vaccine was already available, which was only possible due to concentrated global efforts, and a brand new approach: mRNA (messenger RNA) injections.

Traditional vaccines use a dead or attenuated (weakened) version of the virus in order to trigger a response in the body, without any of the real danger associated with catching the disease. This immune response happens because white blood cells recognise the receptors proteins on the virus to be foreign - it did not originally exist in the body. Once a foreign material has been found, immune cells (B-cells) are called to the site of infection, each with a different antibody attachment. These antibodies all have different shapes, meaning they can attach to different receptors proteins including the ones on the virus. The B-cells then go through a selection process to find the B-cell with the corresponding antibody that can bind to the viral receptor (the ‘spike’ protein in Covid), which replicates to create many copies of itself, producing a large amount of the antibody. This binds to the viral receptor proteins, rendering the virus unable to harm other body cells and further replicate. Some of the B-cells become Memory cells, storing the correct antibody so that in case the same virus ever infects the body again, the selection process doesn’t have to be done again. Vaccination gives people immunity to disease, because the virus is recognised and destroyed by the antibodies so quickly that no symptoms appear. There can be minor side effects, like fever or fatigue, but those are usually preferable to the disease that it protects from. However, the problem with traditional vaccines is that they take a long time to make, especially due to the fact that the virus has to be weakened enough as to not cause sickness, but at the same time its surface receptor proteins still have to be recognisable to the immune system as a threat. Finding the right balance is time-consuming, which forced scientists to switch to using mRNA under the tight deadline.

mRNA is a single-stranded version of the DNA found in our bodies, usually used as a template to make essential proteins that carry out body functions. Each mRNA strand carries the instructions to make 1 protein. Researchers first identified the part of the viral genetic code that created the ‘spike’ protein in the Covid virus and inserted it into some bacterial plasmid (ring-shaped) DNA, which is then cut to form a linear string of DNA. This is then used to synthesise a strand of mRNA which carries the ‘code’ for the Covid protein. Finally, the mRNA was purified and used as the basis of the vaccine. When injected, it stimulates our own body cells to make its own harmless ‘spike’ proteins using the mRNA template, which then triggers the immune response. This results in Memory cells that are able to recognise and fight the Covid virus without us even being in physical contact with it at all.

Other than saving months of time and billions of pounds by making our bodies grow the viral receptor proteins rather than in a lab, it also avoids the need for adjuvants (substances that boost the immune response to the proteins) and therefore skips the entire process of testing for the optimal amount to add to the vaccine, saving even more time. This is because mRNA already naturally attracts immune cells due to its regular purpose in the human body.

Furthermore, the short production time for mRNA vaccines means that more successful vaccines can be made. Viruses often mutate and change their genetic code, sometimes to the point of creating a new variant of the disease. More than 26 variants of Covid have been identified since 2020, with the Delta and Omicron variants being the most deadly strains worldwide and the BA.3.2 strain being the most recent variant arriving in the UK. Traditional vaccines are unable to keep up with the rate of mutation and change in these viruses due to the long modification and cultivation procedures needed before mass production, hence why there is a new flu shot every year. However, using mRNA, scientists simply have to swap out the old protein sequence for a new variation in order to make a whole new version of the vaccine, greatly simplifying the process.

In the past, one of the problems preventing mRNA vaccines from being manufactured was that it didn't have a stable structure, making it prone to breakage. Moreover, ribonucleases (specialised proteins) would break down the injected free-floating mRNA before it could even be made into the ‘spike’ proteins because the mRNA itself was seen as foreign by the white blood cells, causing inflammatory responses. In order to overcome this, a ‘cap’ was added to both ends of the mRNA strand. This not only made it resemble human mRNA more, preventing the ribonucleases from attacking it, but also increased its molecular stability, strengthening the bonds within it and keeping it intact.

Unfortunately, there are still some barriers to mass-producing mRNA vaccines, for example the fact that it has to be stored and transported at low temperatures to prevent deterioration, making it more expensive and harder to deliver. Additionally, mRNA vaccines are still a relatively new biotechnology, and studies into their long term adverse effects in humans are still being carried out. One concern is that the mRNA could become integrated into our human DNA, which could have unknown consequences to our health. Another disadvantage, mRNA vaccines always require several booster shots in order to maintain immunity, while some traditional vaccines can offer lifelong immunity after just 1 shot.

Despite all these problems, mRNA is still a major medical advancement. The Covid pandemic was the launchpad that proved that this biotechnology could be applied in a real life setting, producing an effective vaccine that was used to save millions of lives around the globe in a short amount of time. Its success increased funding for further research into mRNA treatments, such as making personalised cancer therapies. In fact, there have been over 100 clinical trials for cancer patients so far, showing promising results. Vaccines were just the beginning for mRNA application - it will revolutionise the future of medicine, both in prevention and treatment.
