Engineering The Invisible - The Power Of Aerodynamics

By Kelly Aggrey-Fynn · Age 16 (Senior Category) · Dartford Grammar School For Girls · February 20, 2026

Have you ever put your hand outside a moving car's window? The breeze is soft at first, moving between your fingers. However, that light breeze turns into a constant pressure as the car accelerates. The force changes if you tilt your hand slightly; the resistance increases if you flatten it. It's odd how something that we can't see, touch, or even detect when we're motionless can seem so substantial. Why does something invisible feel so strong? The answer lies in aerodynamics: the science of how air moves and how it exerts forces on the objects that pass through it.

Aerodynamics is the science that studies the movement of fluids and the way solid bodies, such as aircraft, move through them. In simpler terms, aerodynamics refers to the movement of air and the way that objects move through air.

Basic concepts

To delve deeper into the world of aerodynamics, it is essential to understand the fundamental principles that govern it: Bernoulli’s principle and Newton’s laws of motion. As introduced earlier, imagine placing your hand outside a moving car and tilting it slightly upward. As the car accelerates, the air splits around your hand. The air travelling over the top of your hand moves faster than the air beneath it. According to Bernoulli’s principle, as the velocity of a fluid increases, its pressure decreases. This means the faster moving air above your hand creates lower pressure, while the slower air below creates higher pressure, producing a net upward force. Newton’s laws further clarify this effect. His first law states that an object remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by an unbalanced force; in this case, the upward force from the air changes the motion of your hand. His second law explains that the amount that an object accelerates depends on the magnitude of the force and the mass of the object. Finally, Newton’s third law states that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. As your tilted hand deflects the air downwards, the air exerts an equal and opposite upward force on your hand. Together, these principles describe how differences in pressure and force interactions combine to generate lift.

There are four fundamental forces which act on moving objects: lift, drag, thrust and weight. Lift acts perpendicular to the direction of motion. Weight is the force of gravity acting downward. Thrust propels the object forward, while drag opposes this motion, resisting its movement through the air. Drag provides resistance, making it harder to move. The more air that hits a surface, the greater the drag produced. Importantly, drag increases with speed; in fact, if velocity doubles, drag increases fourfold. There are two types of drag: friction drag and form drag. Although air often feels vacant, it is made up of billions of tiny particles moving randomly in every direction. When a car or aircraft moves forward, it must constantly push these particles out of the way. This collision between the moving object and the layers of air creates friction drag. As the moving object gains speed, the more turbulent the air becomes as there is greater friction, resulting in form drag. As air flows around it, differences in pressure can create turbulence, increasing resistance. These behaviours are closely linked to patterns of airflow known as laminar flow and turbulent flow. Laminar flow occurs when air moves smoothly in parallel layers, reducing drag, whereas turbulent flow is irregular and chaotic, increasing resistance and causing the object to move slower. Understanding and controlling these airflow patterns is crucial in aerodynamic design, as enhanced shapes minimise drag and improve efficiency.

Cars

Have you ever wondered why most cars aren’t box shaped anymore? The shape of an object affects the amount of drag. Round surfaces usually have less drag than flat ones. Narrow surfaces usually have less drag than wide ones. Streamlined car designs reduce air resistance, which increases speed and reduces the energy and increases fuel efficiency. By controlling airflow around the car, aerodynamic elements like diffusers, air dams, and spoilers increase downforce and lower drag. Race cars, such as those in Formula, as well as supercars, frequently have these aerodynamic characteristics. Downforce, often known as negative lift, is used by the majority of automobiles. The aerodynamic grip produced by downforce enhances the mechanical grip produced by the car's weight, suspension, and tires. Downforce obeys Bernoulli’s principle. Downforce creates an aerodynamic grip which increases the mechanical grip created by tyres, suspension and car’s weight. All of these grips combined, improve the control and manoeuvrability of a vehicle which enhanced control to drivers. Downforce and drag are intrinsically linked. For race cars, downforce is essential for cornering and stability, however too much downforce creates an excessive amount of drag which limits the vehicle’s speed performance. Inverted wings generate a lot of downforce, as they push the car to the ground, a common feature of Formula 1 cars.

Aircrafts

The shape of aircrafts is built so that the lift is great enough to overcome gravity and allow the aircraft to propel. The design of aircraft wings, fuselages and tails are all based on aerodynamic principles to ensure optimal lift, minimal drag and maximum fuel efficiency. The study of bird flight has led to the development of efficient wing designs. An airfoil (aircraft wing) produces lift by generating a pressure difference. Whenever an airfoil is producing lift, the pressure on the lower surface of it is greater than that on the upper surface, which ultimately pushes it upwards. The angled extension on the ends of airfoils are called winglets, which are utilised to reduce drag. The curvature of a plane’s body is also based on Bernoulli’s principle.

Engineering the Invisible

From the steady push against a hand placed outside of a car window to the wings of an aircraft cutting through the sky, aerodynamics demonstrates that even something invisible is full of force. Through Bernoulli’s principle and Newton’s laws, what once felt like a mystery becomes a balance of pressure, motion and reaction. By understanding how air flows and how forces interact, engineers transform something unseen into something controlled and purposeful. In the art of mastering the movement of air, we learn to move with it.
