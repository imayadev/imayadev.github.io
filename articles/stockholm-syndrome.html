Stockholm Syndrome

By Anuva Khan and Scarlett Lau · Ages 12 (Junior Category) · Dame Alice Owen's School · 20th March 2026

What is Stockholm Syndrome?

You might have heard of Stockholm Syndrome. But what is it exactly? Also known as Kidnapper Empathy Syndrome, Stockholm Syndrome is an extremely rare psychological response to being abused, resulting in the victim of the predicament being attached with their captor.

How was Stockholm Syndrome discovered?

Nils Bejerot was the Swedish criminologist and psychiatrist who invented the term to provide a reasonable explanation for the behaviour of 4 hostages. In 1973, an attempted bank robbery was located in Stockholm, Sweden by the convict Jan-Erik Olsson. When released, the 4 people refused to testify against Olsson and had no hatred towards him, defending his actions. It is said Olsson also formed an emotional bond with the captives. This condition can also be identified as the Norrmalmstorg Syndrome, named after the location of the original robbery.

How do people with Stockholm Syndrome act?

You may be familiar with the classic Disney story of Beauty and the Beast. Whilst a heart-warming story, this actually depicts an example of the coping mechanism. Belle gradually forms an attachment to the Beast despite the fact that he contained her father, then her in exchange. This shows how the victim rules out all the bad deeds committed by the abuser and starts to believe that they are a good, moral person at heart. Other examples include Natasha Kampusch. She was abducted in 1998 at a young age. When her abductor died, she saved a picture of him inside her wallet. Another brainwashed victim was Patty Hearst, taken in by a terrorist group in the year 1974. Later on, she joined the same group.

What are the symptoms?

There is a variety of warning signs such as :

  • Compassion for captor
  • Believing that the abuser did no harm
  • Rising depression along with aggression/frustration
  • Sleeplessness
  • Hopelessness
  • Feeling guilt
  • Constant regret
  • Rumination
  • Having PTSD - Post traumatic stress disorder
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Irritability
  • Vivid flashbacks, nightmares
  • Aggressive behaviour
  • Needing their abductor and relies on them

Can the victims be manipulated?

It would be possible for the captors to do so and this may happen in relationships with sexual, physical, verbal and domestic abuse. This can go as far as the sufferer gaslighting themselves to reduce tension[cognitive dissonance]. Emotions associated with this could be anxiety, longing for more connection and immense loyalty. The abuser would first offer small acts of benevolence/mercy which encourages their audience to depend on them emotionally. They would also cut off most of their support system, making them the only option to reach for. Most occasions, the victim complies with this as a survival tactic in order to prevent danger towards them. However not every hostage situation is like this. Trauma bonding is not common, probably taking place in 5% to 8% of the occurrences.

Is this long-term?

The Stockholm Syndrome is a unique condition which has no set time and the aftermath effects may be lasting. Even after being removed from the situation, victims may have deep guilt, flashbacks and have their thoughts occupied due to it, which can lead to other related mental issues in the future , for example Post-Trauma Stress Disorder. It can also mean the victim is more vulnerable to all types of abuse than previously. Psychotherapy and Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy could aid in one’s journey to recovery from the trauma but may prove to be difficult. Furthermore, a victim of Stockholm Syndrome, Patty Hearst, even had difficulty recalling her memories from before her joining her captors and had a decreased IQ [130->112].

Can people with Stockholm syndrome affect others and themselves?

They can alienate those who try to help them such as the authorities, family and friends. Sometimes, they may even start to copy their abuser, creating the same environment for others that they were put in [30%-40% of abused children go on to become abusers]. Victims may be so involved that they try to relentlessly change others point of view to match their perspective. If the victim is sadly a child, this can also affect their development.

FAMOUS CASES

Examples include Natasha Kampusch. She was abducted in 1998 at a young age. When her abductor died of suicide, she cried upon learning his death and saved a picture of him inside her wallet. Another brainwashed victim was Patty Hearst, taken by the terrorist group SLA in the year 1974. Later on, she joined the group and committed crimes as part of SLA. Mary McElroy was a victim, kidnapped in 1933. When rescued from her captors, she claimed that they were just normal business men and visited them in jail. Later on, she committed suicide with a note left behind saying - ‘My four captors were the only people on Earth that saw me and never thought of me as a fool’.

What factors increase the risk of Stockholm Syndrome?

Some pre-existing factors can increase the risk of Stockholm Syndrome. Some examples of these are prolonged exposure to stress, low self-esteem, and a history of neglect/abuse. Individuals who have experienced trauma bonding and codependency before have a higher risk of having Stockholm Syndrome. Another factor can be the view of society. For example, men experiencing abuse projected by women may be afraid to speak up as they are usually seen as the abusers in relationships. Stockholm Syndrome may also occur due to both the victim and the captor sharing an interest - commonly survival.

How intense can Stockholm Syndrome get?

The intensity can vary based on the predicament and the personality of the victim. There can be 3 types - low, medium and high.

LOW - feels protection on some occasions but is still wary of danger,minimising abuse (It really wasn’t that bad)

MEDIUM - blames themselves or others for the abuse, doubt that authorities have their best interests at heart, gratitude increases for the abuser

HIGH - refusal to abandon captor, deep rooted love / loyalty, difficulty to distinguish between abuser and captor

How can Stockholm Syndrome be prevented in future generations?

There are many possible factors to this, but one of the main controllable environmental factors identified by psychologists was exposure to trauma. Research found that while 8% of normal victims suffered from Stockholm syndrome, this number is much higher amongst abused children. This can be a factor to consider in the future.

How is the discovery of Stockholm Syndrome important?

Stockholm Syndrome helped psychologists understand paradoxical trauma bonding and for evolutionary psychologists to know more about survival coping mechanisms and cognitive dissonance [when a person holds 2+ conflicting beliefs and rationalizes one to lessen the tension .It also helps with improving therapy experiences and their effectiveness, like the application of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy [CBT - a type of therapy designed to change maladaptive thought patterns and behaviour]. Furthermore, it has political influence as Stockholm Syndrome can “brainwash” victims into crime. Patty Hearst[as mentioned earlier] was pardoned by Jimmy Carter after having her being “manipulated” taken into mind.

This is a serious condition that should not be joked about. Despite the severity of this, it is very rare for victims to obtain Stockholm Syndrome. Spread the awareness to everyone you know. They may be the next one…
