Turning Back the Cellular Clock: The Science of Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells

By Alexander Schauer · Age 17 (Senior Category) · Alsager Sixth Form · 14th March 2026

Our bodies contain around 37 trillion cells (1), from T and B cells which defend against pathogenic invaders to the nerve cells which transmit the electric impulses from our brains and spinal cords. Yet every one of us began life as a single cell known as a zygote, formed from the fusion of an egg and sperm cell from our mothers and fathers. It therefore becomes almost incomprehensible to think that this single cell goes on to form all 37 trillion in an adult human body – a number roughly 90 times the number of stars in the Milky Way galaxy (2). How can our bodies carry out such diverse functions? How are the specialised cells which enable these functions produced? The answer lies in stem cells.

Stem cells have two defining properties: potency, the ability to differentiate and become other cell types, and self-renewal, the ability to divide repeatedly to form new stem cells. While stem cells are abundantly found in embryos in order for human development to occur, in adults they are only found in certain tissue types such as the bone marrow and crypts of the small and large intestine (3). However, not only do stem cells differ in where they are found but also in their potency – the range of cells they are able to produce. Potency exists as a spectrum, from totipotent cells in the early embryo, capable of forming all embryonic and extra-embryonic tissues, to multipotent adult stem cells which are capable only of generating the specialised cells in the tissues where they are found. Between these extremes are the pluripotent stem cells which can give rise to every cell type in the body apart from the placenta (4). It is to these pluripotent stem cells that our attention will now turn.

Before we can understand how induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be created, we must first examine how gene expression determines the identity of a cell. Although every somatic cell contains the same DNA sequence, it is the expression of particular genes which dictate the properties of the cell, with different cell subsets expressing different genes. Which genes are expressed is controlled by transcription factors, proteins which bind to genes and switch them ‘on or off’. It is the expression of particular genes which differentiates a skin fibroblast from a nerve cell, and a nerve cell from a pluripotent stem cell (5).

Previously, scientists believed that the process of cell specialisation was a permanent process. However, in 2006 a ground-breaking discovery by Nobel Prize winning scientist Shinya Yamanaka demonstrated that by introducing particular transcription factors, known as Yamanaka factors, to specialised cells, they can be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). In order to reset the cell’s gene expression program and cause the cell to lose its specialised identity, the four factors Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc are introduced into the cell before they act in the nucleus, modifying which genes are expressed by activating pluripotency genes and suppressing those responsible for specialisation. In doing so, Yamanaka achieved the previously unimaginable feat of restoring a specialised cell to its previous undifferentiated, stem-cell state, capable of differentiating into numerous other types of cell (6).

The ability to reprogram specialised cells into a pluripotent state has undoubtedly opened a new frontier in medicine, allowing doctors and scientists to create patient-specific stem cells capable of differentiating in order to potentially repair damaged tissues (7). In the future, iPSCs could be used to treat patients with type I diabetes by differentiating into insulin-producing β-cells for the pancreas (8), or even aid in treating heart failure by repairing damaged heart tissue caused by myocardial infarction (heart attacks) (9). Additionally, as specialised cells derived from patient-specific iPSCs will have the same DNA as all other cells in the patient’s body, the risk of transplanted cells being rejected by the immune system falls dramatically compared with standard transplantation alongside which immunosuppressants have to be used. Unlike other transplanted cells which will have slightly different DNA sequences than other cells in the host’s body, specialised cells produced from iPSCs will display the antigens recognised by the immune system as ‘self’, potentially drastically increasing the success rate of tissue transplants.

Despite their enormous potential in a wide range of medical applications, iPSCs still have several challenges and risks to their use which still need to be addressed by further research. One major concern is that some of the transcription factors used to reprogram specialised cells – particularly c-Myc – may be associated with an increased cancer risk, potentially leaving patients who receive iPSC therapy with the further need for oncological treatment. As c-Myc is a well-known oncogene, a gene which can transform a cell into a tumour cell, if it is overexpressed during reprogramming it can drastically increase the risk of uncontrolled cell division, a hallmark of cancer (10). In addition, one of the main barriers to the widespread integration of iPSCs into clinical treatment is the low proportion of cells which undergo successful reprogramming, with only around 0.01-0.1% of cells successfully being reprogrammed into iPSCs (11). This difficulty primarily arises due to specialised cells having stabilised epigenetic modifications which control their gene expression and hence must be erased during cellular reprogramming. As a consequence of this, the majority of cells fail to fully activate the genes required to induce pluripotency, causing the process to be both time- consuming and technically demanding (11). Despite these setbacks, current research into safer reprogramming methods, such as RNA-based delivery methods of Yamanaka transcription factors, aims to reduce the risk of insertional mutations and cancer development, while reprogramming efficiency aims to be improved by optimising culture conditions, for example by introducing histone-modifying proteins (12).

It is undeniable that the discovery that specialised cells can be reprogrammed into an induced pluripotent state has transformed our understanding of cellular identity and gene expression, effectively allowing scientists to reset the developmental clock of a cell by introducing specific transcription factors. Induced pluripotent stem cells have opened up a world of new possibilities for regenerative medicine and tissue transplantation, allowing patient-specific stem cells to be developed with the ability to differentiate into a range of specialised cells. As scientists continue to develop this technology, the ability to reprogram cells may ultimately transform the way in which we view disease and treat previously incurable diseases as iPSCs begin to play a central role in personalised medicine.
