Does consciousness shape reality? Quantum physics and Wigners radical idea..

By Rim Watili · Age 15 (Senior Category) · Kingsdale Foundation School · 25th February 2026

If a tree were to fall and no one heard, did it actually make a sound? That sounds like a joke but that's the beauty of quantum physics, it becomes serious. At the smallest scales, particles don't behave like tiny solid objects. Instead of having definite positions or properties, they are often described as something called a wavefunction.

A wave function doesn't tell us what something is,it tells us what it could be. It contains nearly infinite probabilities for just as many outcomes.

Before measurement, a quantum particle can exist in something called a superposition: This means multiple states at once. An electron can be “spin up” or “spin down” at the same time. In the famous double slit experiment conducted by Thomas young in 1801, a single particle can behave by passing through two slits at once, at the same time. Crazy right?

Yet when we measure it, we never see both the outcomes but rather only one. Somehow, this sea of possibilities becomes one single reality, which is the one we observe.

This is called the measurement problem. The equation of quantum mechanics, the Schrodinger equation, predicts that the wavefunction unfolds over time. It never says to collapse into one result, but instead predicts a smooth and continuous evolution of all the possible outcomes.

One answer came from Copenhagen interpretation, associated with physicists such as Niels Bohr. According to this view, the wave function collapses when measured. The act of observation decides an outcome.

But what actually counts as measurement? A detector? Camera? Geiger counter? Or maybe it's something more mysterious.

In the 1960s, physicist Eugene Wigner asked an important question which pushes this concept. Imagine a scientist inside a lab measuring a quantum particle. From the scientists point of view, the wave function collapses when they observe the result. But from outside that lab, another observer doesn't know the outcome yet. According to quantum mechanics, the entire lab, the particle, measuring device and even the scientist could be described as being in a superposition.

In other words, from a different point of view the scientist may be in a superposition of saw spin up and saw spin down. This feels silly, we don't experience ourselves and blurry probability.

So Wigner proposed a crazy idea: maybe physical interactions aren't enough alone to cause that collapse. Maybe consciousness, the act of becoming aware, is what finally turns probability to reality.

It was an extraordinary idea. It implied that the mind and matter are not separate, that awareness might be built into the structure of physical law.

However, modern development makes this picture complicated. A process called decoherence explains how quantum systems interacting with their environment lose their superpositions.

When a particle interacts with air molecules, photons and surrounding matter, it becomes entangled with many many other particles. The different possible outcomes basically stop interfering with each other.

To someone observing, it looks like collapse, but mathematically, the wave function may still be evolving without any partake of consciousness. Because of decoherence, most physicists today don't believe that human awareness is required for quantum events to become real outcomes, in fact Wigner himself reconsidered his view.

Yet the fact that such an idea has been seriously debated shows quantum mechanics doesn't just challenge technology, but it also changes our understanding of reality itself.

Another interpretation known as the many worlds interpretation proposed by Hugh Everett removes collapse entirely.

Instead of possibilities disappearing, they all occur, each in a separate branch of the universe. In this, consciousness does not choose outcomes. It simply follows one branch of a constantly branching structure of reality.

At the moment we can't experimentally differ between these interpretations. They make the same predictions, so the debate is what the maths means, not what it predicts.

So does consciousness create reality?

Modern physics doesn't give us enough evidence that it does, but who knows, that might change in the future. Quantum mechanics makes us ask the question in a serious way. It reminds us that reality isn't solid and obvious. It is probabilistic, entangled, mysterious and stranger than common sense allows.

Quantum theory is still an unfinished story. It is possible that in future developments, we may discover deeper connections between observation, consciousness and physical reality. Throughout history, ideas once considered radical have later reshaped science entirely. Whether consciousness plays any important roles in quantum processes remains somewhat unknown, but the thought continues to inspire people every day.
